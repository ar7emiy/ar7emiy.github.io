Nov 22, 2026·Research screener

GraphRAG Zero, GRASP, MAC Bench, GNN-as-Feature-Factory

Here is what stood out this week: evaluation infrastructure for graph-augmented retrieval, a sufficiency check that catches incomplete evidence paths before generation, a controlled measurement of how badly frontier agents coordinate, and a practical hybrid fraud pattern that keeps the production model in place. RGH-GNN reappeared in this week's screener as complementary work to the fraud item; it was covered in depth on October 27, so it is not repeated here.

1. GraphRAG Zero + BenchmarkQED (Microsoft Research, 17 Nov 2026)

Successive generations of GraphRAG have driven indexing and retrieval cost down until an embedding-derived graph can deliver sub-second, LLM-free retrieval of relevant and representative context. BenchmarkQED supplies the matching evaluation machinery, activity-based query synthesis, assertion coverage, cluster recall, topical fidelity, so that efficiency gains can be measured against evidence quality rather than answer fluency alone.

Architecture: the design is best understood as the third step in a cost ladder. First-generation GraphRAG extracted entities and relations with an LLM, built community summaries, and answered global questions by synthesizing across those summaries. LazyGraphRAG, the second generation, deferred all LLM use to query time and cut indexing cost to 0.1 percent of full GraphRAG, but query-time LLM work still left a minute-scale delay before answer generation. GraphRAG Zero removes the LLM from indexing entirely and can be configured to retrieve without LLM calls at query time either. The graph is derived from the geometry of text embeddings, and context assembly is a retrieval operation over that graph rather than a generation step. In the real-time configuration, generative-model processing is reserved for the final response, so answer generation can begin almost immediately after the query arrives. The evaluation side is BenchmarkQED, and its structure matters as much as the system it measures. AutoQ synthesizes queries along two dimensions, source (data-derived or activity-derived) and scope (local or global), producing four classes: data-local, activity-local, data-global, and activity-global. It also generates source-grounded assertions alongside the questions, using the same material to establish what an answer should contain, so assertions become testable requirements rather than post-hoc judgments. AutoE then supports two scoring modes: pairwise LLM-as-judge comparisons, and assertion-based scoring that checks how many of the required assertions the answer covers. Retrieval is measured separately from answers, with chunk precision judged by an LLM over source chunks and recall measured at the topical-cluster level, which captures breadth of coverage rather than conventional document-level recall. The loop-engineering detail is worth noting: datasets, questions, assertions, and judging procedures are held fixed within a cycle, and cached assertion-chunk judgments are reused across system versions, with new judgments needed only for previously unassessed evidence, so repeated retrieval testing stays economical while preserving a common basis for measuring progress and catching regressions.

The evaluation ran 200 synthetic questions, split equally between multi-hop and global, over a collection of nearly 1,400 news articles plus podcast transcripts. At a 50-chunk retrieval budget, global answer assertion coverage rose from 53.4 percent with vector RAG to 57.4 percent with GraphRAG Zero, using the same embedding model and answer generator and one response per query. Context assembly averaged 0.93 seconds including query embedding and excluding answer generation. GraphRAG Zero achieved higher cluster recall and topical fidelity, while vector RAG retained higher chunk precision. The authors are explicit that these are research evaluations, not Foundry IQ production measurements. GraphRAG Zero is now available as an option in Foundry IQ (Azure AI Search), and GitHub's Repo Mind Light ships with it for repository understanding over issues and pull requests. Optional reasoning extensions improve global answer coverage further at the cost of additional model processing. The original GraphRAG paper now has 3.9k citations since April 2024, and the open-source repository has over 36k GitHub stars. External technical reaction so far is thin beyond Microsoft channels, though practitioners integrating with Foundry IQ have noted the release.

What I'd borrow: claims, policies, FNOL narratives, and medical notes are long, partially templated, and require both local fact lookup and global pattern and risk synthesis across a file or portfolio. The failure mode of classic GraphRAG was indexing cost and latency that made continuous update impractical; Zero removes that barrier for interactive agent use. The evaluation machinery is the part I would take most seriously. Assertion coverage, cluster recall, and topical fidelity measure evidence quality directly, and the fixed-standard loop with cached judgments is exactly the discipline a claims retrieval system needs if it is going to be iterated on without silently regressing. What must be true for transfer: entity and relation extraction quality on insurance-specific schemas, parties, injuries, coverages, exclusions, provider codes, must be high enough that the embedding-derived graph preserves the distinctions that matter, otherwise cluster recall gains will be on the wrong topical structure. Assertion coverage is useful, but insurance still needs provenance back to the exact clause or note span and auditable negation and assertion status, which this evaluation does not yet provide. I would also want to see the 53.4 to 57.4 percent coverage gain reproduced on claims corpora before treating it as a decision input, since a four-point gain on synthetic podcast and news questions is a signal, not a guarantee.

2. GRASP: Query-Adaptive Graph Retrieval with Path Sufficiency Verification (arXiv 2611.19441, 14 Nov 2026)

Edge relevance and a learned stopping gate are not sufficient. A retrieved path can contain relevant edges yet miss a necessary relation. GRASP adds a path-level sufficiency classifier that triggers bounded re-retrieval when evidence is predicted incomplete, reducing unsupported claims before generation.

Architecture: the framework runs in five stages, labelled A through E, and the central idea is that insufficiency is a retrieval problem to be fixed before generation, not a generation problem to be papered over afterward. Stage A encodes the input query into representations that guide retrieval. Stage B constructs the candidate evidence path. A cross-attention scorer models fine-grained interactions between individual query tokens and candidate edges, giving query-specific signals for edge selection rather than a single embedding similarity per edge. A learned stopping gate conditions traversal termination on the query, the traversal state, and the hop depth, so retrieval depth adapts to the current state instead of stopping at a fixed hop count. Stage B is trained with hop-level supervision. Stage C evaluates whether the assembled path is structurally sufficient to support answering the query. It encodes the ordered retrieval path and combines path-level information with retrieval confidence statistics: edge scores, hop-level score margins, candidate uncertainty, and path length. Stage C is trained separately, using confidence statistics produced by the converged retrieval model. Stage D decides whether the path is accepted or another retrieval attempt is initiated. When the path is predicted insufficient, GRASP runs a bounded re-retrieval procedure: it identifies a low-confidence retrieval action using its hop-level score margin, excludes that action in subsequent attempts, and restarts traversal from the entry entity to seek an alternative path. Once a path is accepted, Stage E uses the retrieved evidence as input to the language model to generate the answer. At inference time the two trained stages interact through this verification and correction loop. Authors are Rui Xu (Karolinska Institutet), Le Wang (KTH), and Tianyue Wang (University of Michigan). License is CC BY-NC-ND.

The evaluation runs on three biomedical question-answering benchmarks with different graph scales and answer lengths: RiTeK, MedKGent-KG, and a PMC-based corpus. The unsupported-claim rate dropped 8.9 to 13.2 percentage points versus the strongest learned baseline, with the largest gain on the benchmark with the longest generated answers. Ablations show the three components, cross-attention scoring, the stopping gate, and the sufficiency classifier, are complementary, and the retrieval gains are more pronounced under higher candidate density, though the authors note that observation alone does not establish a causal link between graph density and performance. Reaction remains thin, primarily the preprint itself, with no independent replication yet.

What I'd borrow: medical notes and claims narratives contain multi-hop clinical reasoning, symptom to diagnosis to treatment to exclusion, and legal multi-hop, clause to condition to party. The failure mode of standard graph retrieval is silent incompleteness: the path looks relevant but misses a critical negation or temporal constraint, and the generator fills the gap. GRASP treats that as a retrieval problem, which I think is the right framing. The sufficiency classifier is the transferable mechanism, and the re-retrieval correction loop is the part that makes it actionable rather than diagnostic. What must be true for transfer: the underlying knowledge graph must be extracted with high relation precision and typed edges that match an insurance ontology, not free-text biomedical relations, and the sufficiency classifier must be calibrated on insurance-style incomplete paths, a missing exclusion, a missing laterality, a missing date range. Biomedical graphs are denser and cleaner than raw claims text graphs, so entity resolution noise will inflate false sufficiency or force excessive re-retrieval, and the re-retrieval budget has to be bounded tightly or the latency cost eats the reliability gain. I would prototype this as a retrieval-stage gate in front of an existing claims GraphRAG, with the classifier trained on deliberately corrupted paths, before trusting it in production.

3. Multi Agent Coordination Bench (MAC Bench) (Bespoke Labs, 18-19 Nov 2026)

Frontier agents still coordinate poorly. A controlled comparison on twelve long-horizon, natively multi-agent tasks, shared versus distributed information and actions, same goal, shows that eight-agent teams beat a single agent mainly because there are more workers, not because they message effectively. Silent eight-agent runs often score higher than communicating ones.

Architecture: the benchmark itself is the system worth describing, because its construction is what lets it isolate coordination from parallelism. Twelve tasks run in simulated environments, each built so that one agent's work depends on its teammates'. Tasks are categorized by two properties taken from the partially observable stochastic game formalism: whether the environment divides the agents' inputs and outputs, and whether the agents pursue the same goal. In a shared task, all agents start with the same information and the same actions, and a single agent could complete the task alone given enough time. In a distributed task, the environment gives agents different information, different actions, or both, so no agent observes or controls the whole system. Nine of the twelve tasks are shared, including Mars Return, Dubai ATC, and Cave Rescue; three are distributed: Holdout-game, Mindustry, and Power Outage. Commons and strategic tasks, where agents pursue different goals, are excluded from version one, because the coordination gain cannot be measured cleanly when opponents change the task. Each task is run three ways: a single agent, k agents without coordination, and k agents with a shared message board. The coordination gain is the difference between the communicating and silent multi-agent settings, which separates the benefit of messaging from the benefit of simply having more workers. The tasks are long-horizon by design. In Mars Return, the team designs a Mars sample-return mission and writes its flight software in one repository, describing the vehicle in SysML v2, under a hardware budget of 1.6 billion dollars and an R and D budget of 240 million, and the grader flies the committed design through 48 hidden worlds with different atmospheres, winds, dust storms, and hardware variations. In Holdout-game, each agent plays one survivor on a 96 by 96 tile map that never pauses, sees only 7 tiles by day and 4 at night, and communicates through channels that are part of the game: speech reaches 10 tiles, a shout reaches 25 but draws zombies, the noticeboard works only at the stronghold, and the radio reaches the whole map but drains scarce batteries. The silent runs can be verified as truly silent, since the logs confirm no message passed between agents.

Averaged across Opus 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol, and Gemini 4 Argon: a single agent scores 0.35, a silent team of eight scores 0.44, and a communicating team of eight scores 0.42. Only Opus 5.5 shows a coordination gain, reaching 0.53 as a team. Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol score about the same alone, but in teams of eight Opus 5.5 reaches 0.53 and GPT-6.1 Sol only 0.41. Teams cost about 8.8 times as much as a single agent and reach the single-agent score only about 2.2 times faster. In Mars Return, averaged over the three models, a team of eight scores 0.26 against a single agent's 0.21 and the best of eight single agents at 0.38, and Opus 5.5 scores 0.57 with a team of four but 0.33 with a team of eight, so beyond some team size the cost of extra writers to the same files and extra claims on the same budgets outweighs the extra work. Harness and tasks are released on GitHub. Reaction is concentrated on X and the benchmark site; credible practitioners are noting the coordination gap rather than celebrating team architectures.

What I'd borrow: specialized coworker and multi-agent systems are attractive for claims triage, one agent for medical review, one for coverage, one for fraud signals, one for summary. The failure mode this benchmark names is that communication overhead and state-tracking failures erase the parallelism gain, or agents overwrite each other's structured extractions. The measurement design is what I would take into product evaluation: run the silent multi-agent setting as a control, and only claim a coordination benefit if the communicating team beats it. What must be true for transfer: the task must have a genuine information or action partition, for example only the medical agent sees the full clinical note and only the coverage agent can query the policy graph, plus a shared scarce resource or interface contract that forces coordination. Otherwise the measured result predicts that independent parallel extraction followed by a single merger step will be cheaper and at least as accurate. The Mars Return team-size result also argues for small teams with owned parts and agreed interfaces rather than large flat teams, and for treating the message board as a cost to be budgeted, not a free capability.

4. GNN-as-Feature-Factory + XGBoost for Network Fraud (Thoughtworks / AWS, 19 Nov 2026)

Instead of replacing the production gradient-boosted model, use a GNN only to produce node embeddings that encode relational risk, shared devices, overlapping IPs, coordinated timing, then concatenate those embeddings with classic tabular features for the final decision. This surfaces collective anomalies without requiring a full rewrite of the scoring stack.

Architecture: the reference architecture is built on the NVIDIA AI Blueprint for fraud detection, deployed as an AWS sample on Amazon SageMaker, and it has four parts plus a phased adoption path. First, graph construction: the financial system is modeled as a graph of users, devices, merchants, accounts, and IP addresses, stored in Amazon Neptune for real-time retrieval. Second, the GNN as a feature factory: message passing aggregates information from each node's neighbors, and those neighbors aggregate from theirs, propagating risk signals across multiple hops and producing a dense node embedding that encodes where an entity sits in the network and who it is connected to. The embeddings are concatenated with traditional tabular features and fed into XGBoost, which remains the final decision model. Third, the real-time inference pipeline: transaction details stream in through a message bus such as Apache Kafka, the system queries Neptune for a localized k-hop subgraph around the transaction, runs GNN inference on that subgraph with NVIDIA GPU acceleration, assembles the feature vector, and XGBoost outputs a probability score, all within the sub-100 millisecond window real-time payments require. Subgraph sampling is what makes the latency target reachable at scale. Fourth, orchestration and monitoring on SageMaker, with model monitoring, retraining triggers, and drift detection built in. The phased path is deliberately incremental. Phase 1 builds the graph offline and runs the GNN in shadow mode to validate F1 uplift without touching live traffic. Phase 2 injects batch-generated GNN embeddings into the live production system. Phase 3 advances to full real-time graph querying and low-latency GNN inference at the point of transaction. Complementary academic work, RGH-GNN (arXiv 2610.27107, 19 Oct), reaches AUC-ROC 0.914 on IEEE-CIS by using LightGBM risk scores as edge weights in a risk-gated heterogeneous aggregation, which supports the same conclusion from the research side: engineered risk signals help combat dilution under camouflage.

On the IBM TabFormer synthetic credit-card dataset, 24 million transactions, the ensemble improved F1 by about 15 percent over standalone XGBoost and reduced false-positive alerts by about 20 percent. The authors note these are offline benchmark results on synthetic data, the same validation approach institutions use in Phase 1 shadow scoring, and that institutions running the open-source sample can reproduce the results and calibrate thresholds against their own transaction distributions before advancing. Practitioner reaction is positive in the AWS Partner blog; the academic risk-gated variants remain mostly uncited outside the preprint.

What I'd borrow: claims and provider fraud graphs are heterogeneous, claimant to provider to policy to device to address, and class-imbalanced. Pure GNNs often lose the strong tabular signals, claim amount distribution and historical frequency, that tree models already capture. The failure mode of pure GNN deployment is operational: latency, monitoring, and the need to retrain when the graph schema changes. The hybrid pattern keeps the existing XGBoost, or its equivalent, as the system of record and treats the GNN as an upstream feature store, which is the right shape for a regulated environment. What must be true for transfer: entity resolution across claims, policies, and medical notes must be accurate enough that graph edges are not dominated by false merges; the embeddings must remain stable under the incremental updates typical of daily claims intake; and explainability must still surface the specific neighbors or paths that drove the embedding, through attention or subgraph extraction, because regulators and SIU analysts require it. The phased path is the part I would follow literally: shadow scoring first, then batch embedding injection, and real-time inference only after the embedding stability and explainability questions have answers. A 15 percent F1 lift on synthetic card data is encouraging, but the claims graph is noisier and more heterogeneous, so I would treat the number as a reason to run Phase 1, not as a forecast.

Skipped this week

Several recent conference abstracts on hybrid GNN-Transformer models for explainable financial fraud, from SciTePress and local venues in mid-to-late November, restate the standard message-passing-plus-attention recipe with attention heatmaps and graph sampling. They lack public code, rigorous ablation against strong tabular baselines on realistic insurance-scale graphs, and any independent replication. The claimed scalability and explainability gains are not yet distinguishable from marketing. Time is better spent on the hybrid feature-factory pattern above, or on the risk-gated aggregation results that at least report concrete AUC numbers against GraphSAGE, GAT, and CARE-GNN.
