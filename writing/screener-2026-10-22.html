MEDIATOR, The Last Mile of Coding Agents, LASAGNA

Here is what stood out this week: three new papers on entity resolution, long-document coding agents, and RAG evaluation, with my notes on what might transfer to insurance work. FRACTION also appeared in this week's screener; it was covered in depth on October 20 and this week's treatment adds nothing new, so it is cut under the no-repeat rule.

1. MEDIATOR (arXiv, Oct 2026)

The same clinical concept shows up under different ontology codes, local identifiers, synonyms, or free text across systems, and closed-vocabulary tokenizers treat all of those as unrelated symbols. The cost is concrete: an EHR model trained on MIMIC-III with a closed-vocabulary tokenizer can use only 13% of eICU events, because the rest arrive in other codes or as free text. MEDIATOR (Discrete Neural Interlingua for Ontology-Agnostic EHR Modeling) turns harmonization into open-vocabulary tokenization: instead of maintaining manual mappings between code systems, it learns a shared discrete codebook that any textual expression of a concept can be mapped into. Accepted to NeurIPS 2026; authors are from Mass General Brigham AI, Harvard, and MIT.

Architecture: the tokenizer is a three-stage pipeline sitting in front of a standard EHR Transformer. First, a pretrained text encoder maps any clinical concept expressed as text (an ontology code's description, a local code's description, an uncoded free-text entry) into a dense embedding. Second, a trainable adaptor projects that embedding into a quantization space. Third, the projected vector is assigned to the nearest entry of a fixed-size vector-quantized codebook by cosine similarity, and the codebook itself is maintained with exponential-moving-average updates; each code is a semantic prototype, and the tokenizer's output is simply the discrete code index, which the downstream Transformer consumes like any other token. Training is what shapes the space. The core supervision is cross-ontology synonymy: each underlying concept is treated as an equivalence class of surface forms, and same-concept contrastive objectives are applied both before and after quantization, together with multi-view assignment agreement, so equivalent expressions collapse onto the same code. Three further knowledge sources guide the geometry: enriched concept descriptions, empirical code co-occurrence, and curated relation-graph structure, plus a reconstruction objective back into the encoder's embedding space. At inference there is no alignment step at all: an unseen hospital's codes or free text are encoded and quantized into the same codebook, which is what makes the vocabulary open rather than fixed.

The results separate coverage from representation, which is the honest way to report them. On 20 MIMIC-III tasks, MEDIATOR improves mean AUPRC by 4.0 points (8% relative) over the strongest prior tokenizer. In zero-shot transfer to 14 eICU tasks the gain is 8.6 points (29% relative), using all events rather than 13%. When the inputs are matched so coverage is held equal, it is still the best tokenizer, by +1.0 points on operational tasks and +1.9 on subcohort-mortality tasks, so part of the transfer gain is genuinely better representation, not just more data. Same-encoder controls attribute the in-domain gains mainly to the text encoder, and the transfer robustness and entity-linking quality to the learned objectives. Codebook analysis shows equivalent expressions collapsing while distinct concepts remain separated, and the shared token space also makes attribution transferable across hospitals, since predictions at both sites are defined over the same tokens.

What I'd borrow: claims, policies, FNOL, and medical notes are full of near-synonyms, local codes, and free-text variants of the same entities (diagnoses, procedures, body parts, providers), and cross-source entity resolution today usually means brittle, hand-maintained mapping tables. A discrete, open-vocabulary interlingua is a plausible replacement: new sources get encoded into the shared space instead of triggering another mapping project. The discreteness is itself valuable in a regulated setting, because a token assignment is inspectable in a way a continuous embedding is not. Transfer needs care on three points. (a) The synonym and relation supervision has to cover the lexical variation and hierarchical structure actually present in claims text; otherwise the codebook either over-collapses distinct insurance concepts (a real audit risk) or fails to collapse true equivalents. (b) The method is demonstrated on structured EHR events (codes plus short descriptions), not the long, noisy, templated narrative style of insurance documents, so the encoder's behavior on document-length context is unproven. (c) Attribution transfer across hospitals is interesting, but whether a collapse decision can be defended when a linkage is challenged in a claims audit is a separate, harder question. Reaction is thin: arXiv and NeurIPS poster listings, no substantial practitioner discussion yet.

2. The Last Mile of Coding Agents (arXiv, Oct 2026)

Coding agents (systems that select tools, execute code, and revise their own approach as they work) are increasingly pointed at long, unstructured documents, but it is unclear how reliably they convert an entire document into accurate structured output. This study uses clinical trial protocol digitization as the testbed: protocols are the source of truth for study design, yet they live as unstructured PDFs, and digitizing them means producing structured, machine-readable data that clinical software can validate and execute.

Architecture: this is an empirical study, so the architecture worth describing is the evaluation harness and the intervention built on top of it. The benchmark, TrialPD-Bench, is 150 expert-annotated public protocol PDFs across four task categories. Nine proprietary and open-weight models are run inside five coding-agent harnesses. Crucially, the measurement goes beyond final-answer accuracy: it scores evidence recall from the agent traces (did the agent actually locate the required evidence in the document?), agent behavior, and cost. That trace-level instrumentation is what lets the authors localize failures instead of just reporting them. The intervention, TrialScribe, is a domain-specific harness layered on a generic coding agent: task skills that encode the digitization task's structure, plus a verification loop that checks the structured output, wrapped around a locally served open-weight model.

The headline finding is a failure localization, not a leaderboard. Even the strongest agents do not fully solve protocol digitization, and the failure is concentrated at the end of the pipeline: agents usually locate the required evidence, then fail in the last mile of transcribing and structuring it. The harness result is the actionable part. TrialScribe improves the locally served open-weight model by 6.2 to 59.4 points across eight metrics, enough for it to match or exceed proprietary models such as GPT-5.6-Sol. In other words, the gap was mostly not model capability; it was task structure and verification around the model.

What I'd borrow: policies, claim forms, medical records, and FNOL narratives are long, templated documents that must become structured fields with high fidelity and auditability, and the last-mile failure (evidence found, then incorrectly structured) is exactly the failure mode that creates downstream data-quality and compliance problems in claims pipelines. The borrowable pattern is the harness, not a model upgrade: domain task skills that encode the target schema, and a verification loop that checks structured output against the located evidence before anything is written downstream. Transfer requirements are real, though. Protocols are relatively standardized scientific documents; insurance text carries more variation, cross-references, exclusions, and legal boilerplate, so the harness has to be rebuilt around insurance schemas, validation rules, and evidence-citation requirements rather than adapted superficially. And the cost and trace analysis, useful as it is, does not yet measure production latency or human-review burden, both of which decide whether an extraction pipeline survives contact with real claim volume. Reaction is thin: early arXiv circulation, limited independent commentary so far.

3. LASAGNA (arXiv, Oct 2026)

Modern RAG pipelines stack many techniques (chunking, embedding, retrieval, reranking, query augmentation, context compression, agentic verification), yet almost every published gain is measured in isolation, one component at a time. Three questions follow, and they are rarely answered: do those gains compose when the techniques are combined, do retrieval quality and answer faithfulness favor the same design choices, and do conclusions from small corpora hold at larger scale?

Architecture: LASAGNA is an open-source framework for controlled RAG evaluation, and its design is the contribution. The pipeline is decomposed into its phases (embedding, chunking, retrieval, reranking, query augmentation, context compression, agentic verification), and components are swapped combinatorially inside a single harness so configurations differ only in the choices under test. The study pre-declares three hypotheses before running, then evaluates 450 configurations across eight benchmarks, with corpora ranging from 5K to 200K documents. For every configuration, retrieval quality and answer faithfulness are measured separately, which is what makes the trade-off between them visible at all.

All three findings cut against standard practice. First, composition disappoints: three individually strong retrieval techniques predicted a +47% combined improvement and delivered only +17% together. Second, the objectives diverge: component gains stack differently for retrieval and for faithfulness, and optimizing one can hurt the other. Third, scale changes the answer: the best composition on a small corpus may not remain best as the corpus grows toward 200K documents. The conclusion is methodological but pointed: gains measured in isolation are unreliable predictors of realistic performance, and new techniques should be tested inside the full surrounding stack.

What I'd borrow: GraphRAG and hybrid retrieval systems for claims knowledge bases, policy interpretation, and medical-record QA are being built exactly this way, by stacking components, each justified by an isolated benchmark number. This paper is concrete evidence that the usual evaluation practice overstates expected gains, and worse, can trade away faithfulness, which is the property that matters most for regulated insurance outputs. The engineering take I would actually adopt: evaluate every candidate technique inside the full production stack, on your own corpus, at production scale, with faithfulness tracked as a separate first-class metric rather than inferred from retrieval scores. The failure mode is domain shift, as usual: the benchmarks are general-domain, while insurance corpora have strong template structure, high redundancy, and strict evidence requirements. The compositionality failures will almost certainly appear, but the optimal stack, and the relative cost of faithfulness against retrieval, has to be re-measured on actual claims and policy collections. Reaction: very early (submitted Oct 21), no substantial external reaction yet.

Skipped this week

Leaderboard updates on medical long-context record review (MLCR-AA, with recent models topping out around a 42% pass rate under stricter, reviewer-style prompting). The updates are primarily prompt and judge refinements on an existing benchmark, the reaction is largely vendor-driven, and they supply little new architecture, ablation, or transfer insight for the templated, multi-document, multi-ontology setting of insurance claims and policies. The underlying problem (long medical records remain hard) is real; the updates are not worth deep analyst time right now.
