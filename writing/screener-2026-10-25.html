UMCR, From Unreliable to Usable

Here is what stood out this week: two papers on medical concept recognition and on why LLM entity extraction fails, with my notes on what might transfer to insurance work. MEDIATOR and FRACTION also appeared in this week's screener; MEDIATOR was covered in depth on October 22 and FRACTION on October 20, and neither treatment adds anything new, so both are cut under the no-repeat rule.

1. UMCR: Universal Medical Concept Recognition through Guideline Integration and Agentic Linking (arXiv, Oct 2026)

Medical concept recognition means extracting spans from text and grounding each one to an entry in a standard ontology. Most existing methods are built for one ontology and one text type: clinical notes, drug labels, social media posts, each with its own vocabulary and conventions. UMCR aims to adapt to any target ontology and any source text without ontology-specific retraining, by compiling the dataset's annotation guidelines into a machine-readable form and giving an agentic linker tools to explore the ontology, rather than relying on the LLM's parametric memory of medical vocabularies. Authors are from LAMA-WeST, Polytechnique Montréal, and Mila.

Architecture: the pipeline splits into offline pre-computation done once per ontology and per-document processing. Stage 0, ontology profiling: each concept name is concatenated with its synonyms and embedded with the lightweight Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B model, and the graph exploration tools are built over UMLS subsets, so the same tooling can serve any ontology that maps into UMLS. Stage 1, guideline compilation: each dataset's annotation guidelines, which range from detailed documents with examples and rules to short instruction sentences, are manually encoded into a unified JSON format holding a short task summary, target mention categories with definitions, ontology constraints expressed as rules, and a linking policy. Stage 2, mention detection: an LLM is prompted to extract spans using the compiled guidelines, and the result is augmented with exact matches between the source text and ontology concept names or synonyms. Stage 3, agentic linking: each mention is passed individually to the LLM along with the compiled guidelines, the graph tools, and the source text, and the model explores the ontology on its own. The tools are search, get_concept, get_parents, get_children, get_siblings, adjust_span, commit, and unlink; the model returns one JSON action per turn with a maximum of 8 actions, and commit and unlink are terminal. Most linked mentions use multiple tool calls, averaging 5.3 per mention. Exact-match mentions from Stage 2 skip agentic linking and go straight to filtering. Stage 4, filtering: each mention-concept pair is passed individually to an LLM that checks whether the pair respects the dataset guidelines and returns a reject motive when it discards one. There is no training data and no fine-tuning anywhere in this pipeline; the only per-ontology cost is the Stage 0 embedding pass, and the only per-dataset cost is writing the compiled guideline.

The evaluation covers the nine datasets of the MedPath benchmark plus the two standard phenotype datasets, BioC-GS and GSC+, eleven configurations in total spanning social media posts, patents, drug labels, PubMed abstracts, hospital discharge notes, and ICU discharge notes, with target ontologies including SNOMED CT, UMLS, MeSH, MedDRA, RxNorm, HPO, and OMIM. UMCR achieves the strongest results overall, with the highest overlap-linking F1 in 7 of the 11 configurations, an average F1 of 0.51, and an average rank of 1.18. It outperforms the supervised systems reported for BioC-GS (0.70 F1) and BC5CDR (0.58 F1) while remaining competitive with the strongest supervised results elsewhere. The exception is CADEC, patient forum posts, where CONORM reaches 0.65 against UMCR's 0.27. The ablations isolate the contributions: compiled guidelines gain about 4 F1 points on average over bare guidelines, replacing agentic linking with a fixed retrieve-then-select path degrades performance, adjust_span gives small mention-detection gains, and the final filtering stage gives larger gains on every dataset except ShAReCLEF, which does not improve beyond Stage 2.

What I'd borrow: claims, medical notes, and FNOL narratives contain mixed clinical concepts that must link to several standards at once (ICD, SNOMED, proprietary injury codes, coverage terms), and the compiled-guideline idea is the part I would take most seriously. Insurance annotation policies already exist as documents; encoding them once into a structured guideline that conditions every pipeline stage is a cleaner contract than prompt folklore, and it makes the extraction behavior auditable against a written policy. The agentic linker with a hard action budget is the right shape for long templated documents, provided the budget is enforced. Transfer needs three things. First, high-quality compiled guidelines, which are rarely available at production scale in insurance, and writing them is real work, not a one-line prompt. Second, UMLS-style hierarchical tools; insurance policy language is more contractual and less hierarchical than clinical vocabularies, so the get_parents and get_siblings tools may have less to grab onto. Third, discipline on exact-match augmentation: on boilerplate-heavy documents it will over-trigger, and the filtering stage has to be strong enough to catch that. If those hold, this is a plausible replacement for per-ontology extraction models. Reaction is thin: arXiv listing, no substantial practitioner discussion yet.

2. From Unreliable to Usable: Decomposing Failure Modes and Confidence Signals in LLM Entity Extraction (arXiv, Oct 2026)

LLM entity extraction from full-length documents is widely reported as unreliable, but "unreliable" is a verdict, not a diagnosis, and it does not say whether the output can still be made usable. This preregistered audit asks what the failures actually consist of and whether confidence signals can turn unreliable output into something deployable. The domain is cyber threat intelligence reports, chosen for measurement quality rather than topical breadth: a public corpus with gold annotations, a published critical audit whose protocol and splits are reused for comparability, and a real deployment need, since security analysts already triage extracted indicators by confidence.

Architecture: this is an evaluation study, so the architecture worth describing is the frozen protocol. The corpus is the public Di Tizio collection: 354 incident campaigns, 315 unique reports, 630 model-report evaluation runs, under the critical audit's campaign-level 70/30 split. Three models are evaluated: two new-generation low-cost models, DeepSeek-V4-Flash and GLM-5.3-Flash, with GPT-6.1-Sol as a flagship-tier full-scope control. All runs use frozen prompts and segmentation capped at 40K tokens with 800-token overlap, temperature-0 extraction with 5-sample self-consistency resampling at temperature 0.7 per segment, and 6,048 segment-level calls on the main table. Scoring uses multiset matching with alias equivalence classes: word and digit boundaries for threat-actor groups, fault-tolerant CVE regexes covering PDF mojibake separators and legacy CAN prefixes, and a conservative 13-value synonym table for attack techniques. Confidence is measured three ways, plus two verifiers: verbalized self-reported 0-100 confidence, self-consistency frequency (the share of 5 resamples reproducing the entity, a zero-extra-cost signal), text frequency (the count of the entity string in the report), and their rank-percentile combination, evaluated against self-verification and a third-party calibrated decision model. The acceptance bars were preregistered before evaluation: a tiering bar (top-30% confidence-bucket precision at least overall precision plus 0.10), an annotation agreement line (Cohen's kappa at least 0.7), and a hypothesis register H1-H6 separating measured claims from pending ones. The failure decomposition rests on a three-tier annotation of a 360-row stratified subsample: L1 textual mention (does the string appear), L2 event relevance (does it belong to this incident, not background or related work), L3 relation correctness (is the binding and attribution right). The second-annotator role was filled by two independent LLMs from vendors outside the evaluated set (kimi-k3, minimax-m3) under a frozen prompt with temperature 0 and golden-item gating; 36 hard disagreements went to a third adjudicator model and 27 borderline items were resolved conservatively toward the model-unfavorable side. Agreement is kappa 0.68 three-class on L2 and 0.79 excluding borderline, meeting the preregistered line, and kappa 0.74 on L3.

The findings are precise enough to act on. First, unreliability persists across tiers: micro precision spans 0.03 to 0.34 against recall of 0.81 to 0.99, verbalized calibration error (ECE) is 0.53 to 0.94 across both splits, and the flagship control shows the same pattern (0.63 to 0.94), so the gap does not close at the flagship tier. Second, the dominant failure is not hallucination: 99.7 to 99.8% of false positives have an explicit textual source in the report, and back-projection of the annotation yields 65.6% background mentions, 19.9% gold-scope gaps, and only 1.2% unsupported candidates. In other words, LLMs over-attend to context; they extract correct strings from the wrong part of the document. Third, confidence ranks but does not verify: zero-cost self-consistency-frequency tiering passes the preregistered top-30% precision-gain bar in all six test-split model-type cells (4 of 6 on train, with both vulnerability cells just under the line), and controlling for text frequency, its partial correlation with correctness is significant in 6 of 6 groups (r 0.30 to 0.43). The zero-cost text-frequency times self-consistency combination wins 3, ties 2, and loses 1 across cells. But relation-level counterfactuals on all 45 targets show that a single misattribution sentence flips high-confidence judgments in 95.6 to 100% of cases, and even a background sentence explicitly marked as unrelated still binds a fake vulnerability in up to 26.7% of them. The deployment boundary the authors draw is the sentence to remember: confidence tiering may set review priorities, never truth.

What I'd borrow: entity extraction over long claims, medical notes, and policies is core infrastructure for everything downstream, and this paper reframes how I would evaluate it. The background-contamination diagnosis maps directly onto template boilerplate: a model that extracts an injury code mentioned in a prior-history section, or a provider named in a copied header, is making exactly this error, and counting it as hallucination would send the repair effort in the wrong direction. The gold-scope gap is equally familiar, since insurance gold standards are often incomplete or scope-mismatched, which inflates apparent false positives. The practical borrow is self-consistency-frequency tiering as a zero-cost review-prioritization tool: it is cheap, it passes a preregistered bar, and it requires no logits. Transfer requirements are real, though. The tiering must be calibrated per corpus and per entity type, since the signal behaves differently for actors versus vulnerabilities versus techniques, and thresholds do not transfer across corpora. Human review capacity has to match the residual false-positive rate, or the tiering just reorders a queue nobody can clear. And the counterfactual result is a hard limit on trust: no confidence signal tested here survives a single adversarial or misattributed sentence, so high-confidence extractions must never feed fraud graphs or linkage decisions without verification. Reaction is thin: a systematic evaluation with no broad discussion yet.

Skipped this week

Risk-Gated Heterogeneous GNN (RGH-GNN) and similar recent financial fraud GNN papers that inject a supervised risk score, for example from LightGBM, as edge weights or gates. The core idea, weighting high-risk edges more heavily, is incremental engineering on IEEE-CIS-style graphs, and it does not address the sparse, multi-hop, ontology-rich, and explainability-heavy requirements of insurance fraud graphs. The evaluation stays within standard AUC lifts (0.914 on IEEE-CIS) without the failure-mode analysis or transfer evidence that would make it decision-relevant for product work, so it is noted here and set aside.
