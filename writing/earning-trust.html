Building AI that earns trust in regulated domains

Most AI demos die the moment they meet a compliance officer. I've spent four years building systems that survived that meeting — fraud detection, claims automation, agentic assistants sitting in front of legal teams. Here's what I keep relearning.

The eval is the product

"The model can probably do this" is a hypothesis, not a plan. Every system I ship gets an evaluation suite before it gets users — RAGAS, DeepEval, held to a hard accuracy bar. The eval isn't overhead. It's the thing that lets you move fast without breaking trust, because you know exactly what changed and whether it got better or worse.

Teams that skip evals don't go faster. They go slower, wrapped in arguments about whether the last change helped.

Domain depth beats model cleverness

In fraud detection, the model is maybe 30% of the system. The rest is knowing how investigators actually work: what a suspicious provider network looks like, which claim notes matter, what "resolved" means to the person closing the case. I spent my time embedded with SIU investigators and legal teams, not tuning hyperparameters.

The best AI engineers I know are anthropologists first. Watch the workflow. The automation opportunities announce themselves.

Feedback loops compound; features don't

The highest-leverage thing I built last year wasn't a model — it was a loop. A legal-team email assistant where every draft-vs-human diff became training signal. The system got measurably better week over week, because expert judgment was wired directly into its improvement cycle.

A feature ships once. A feedback loop ships every week, forever.

Write it down

Charters, strategies, eval reports, root-cause analyses. In regulated domains, the written record is part of the system — it's how you prove the thing works, how the next person understands it, how trust transfers from the builder to the operator. I treat documentation as production code.

The bar isn't "impressive demo." It's "durable, auditable, and measurably improving."

That's the standard I hold my own work to. Everything on this site is built that way — or it's marked as an experiment.
